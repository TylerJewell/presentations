## 1. EU AI Act Article 14: what human oversight must make possible, and who designs it versus who performs it

### Takeaway
Article 14 places the design duty on the **provider**: the high-risk system must be built so that natural persons can oversee it while it is in use, and it must reach the deployer in a state that lets the overseer (a) understand capacities and limits and monitor for anomalies, (b) stay aware of automation bias, (c) interpret output correctly, (d) decline to use, disregard, override or reverse the output, and (e) intervene or halt the system through a "stop" button or similar procedure into a safe state. The **deployer** performs the oversight (Article 26). For remote biometric identification under Annex III point 1(a), no action may be taken on a match unless **at least two competent natural persons** have separately verified and confirmed it. Article 14 has not been amended by the Digital Omnibus, but its application date moved to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I).

### Cited Findings
- **14(1), design duty.** High-risk systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use." — [OJ mirror, Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/); legal text: [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **14(2), purpose.** Oversight "shall aim to prevent or minimise the risks to health, safety or fundamental rights" that may emerge in intended use or "under conditions of reasonably foreseeable misuse", in particular where risks persist despite the other Chapter III requirements. — [OJ mirror, Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- **14(3), proportionality and the two routes.** Measures "shall be commensurate with the risks, level of autonomy and context of use". They are delivered through either or both of: (a) measures identified and **built into the system by the provider** before market placement, where technically feasible; (b) measures **identified by the provider** before market placement that are "appropriate to be implemented by the deployer". — [OJ mirror, Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- **14(4), the five overseer capabilities.** The provider must deliver the system so that the natural persons assigned to oversight can, "as appropriate and proportionate":
  - (a) "properly understand the relevant capacities and limitations" and "duly monitor its operation, including in view of detecting and addressing anomalies, dysfunctions and unexpected performance";
  - (b) "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output" ("automation bias"), in particular for systems that give information or recommendations for decisions taken by natural persons;
  - (c) "correctly interpret the high-risk AI system's output, taking into account, for example, the interpretation tools and methods available";
  - (d) "decide, in any particular situation, not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output";
  - (e) "intervene in the operation of the high-risk AI system or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state."
  — [OJ mirror, Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- **14(5), two-person rule.** For Annex III point 1(a) (remote biometric identification), no action or decision may be taken by the deployer on the basis of an identification "unless that identification has been separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons with the necessary competence, training and authority." This does not apply to law enforcement, migration, border control or asylum uses where Union or national law considers it disproportionate. — [OJ mirror, Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)
- **No Omnibus amendment to Article 14.** The mirror shows no amendment marker on Article 14, while it does mark amendments on Articles 4 and 27. — [OJ mirror, Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/); compare [Art. 4](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/4/) and [Art. 27](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/27/)
- **How the provider hands oversight to the deployer.** Article 13(3)(d) requires the instructions for use to state "the human oversight measures referred to in Article 14, including the technical measures put in place to facilitate the interpretation of the outputs". Article 26(1) then requires deployers to use the system "in accordance with the instructions for use". — [Statute, not re-opened] [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 13 and 26](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); Art. 26(1) confirmed at [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **Evidence of oversight in the logs (biometric case).** Article 12(3)(d) requires remote biometric identification systems to log "the identification of the natural persons involved in the verification of the results, as referred to in Article 14(5)". This is the only place where the Act names the overseer's identity as a mandatory log field. — [Statute, not re-opened] [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 12](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **No harmonised standard on human oversight yet.** The Commission's standardisation request C(2025) 3871 covers ten areas including human oversight, record-keeping and logging. Only EN 18286 (quality management) has been cited in the Official Journal (Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/2274, OJ 14 Oct 2026). — [Orrick, Oct 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/10/First-Harmonised-Technical-Standard-Published-Under-the-EU-AI-Act) [Secondary]; [EUR-Lex, Decision 2026/2274](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2026/2274/oj) [Primary, excerpt seen in search]. JTC 21 says work continues on logging, human oversight and accuracy, with the amended request running to 28 Feb 2027. — [jtc21.eu](https://jtc21.eu/); [CEN-CENELEC newsletter](https://www.cencenelec.eu/news-events/news/2026/newsletter/ots-73-etuc/) [Search-summary only]
- **No Commission guideline specifically on Article 14.** Searches found draft guidelines on high-risk classification (19 May 2026) and on Article 50 transparency (consultation to 3 June 2026), but none on Article 14. — [Commission draft high-risk guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/draft-commission-guidelines-classification-high-risk-ai-systems); [Agence Europe on transparency consultation](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13865/15/european-commission-opens-consultation-on-draft-guidelines-on-ai-transparency-obligations) [Search-summary only]

### Inferences
- The split is design versus operation. The provider owes the **capability** (14(4) a–e, built in or specified). The deployer owes the **people**: named natural persons with competence, training, authority and support (26(2)). A centralised oversight function across ten countries is the deployer side. It can only exercise (d) and (e) if the provider shipped override and halt mechanisms, so procurement contracts should require 14(4)(d)–(e) to be demonstrable.
- 14(4)(e) ("stop" button, halt in a safe state) maps directly onto agent kill-switches. 14(4)(d) ("in any particular situation ... disregard, override or reverse") requires per-decision override, not only system-level shutdown.
- 14(3)'s "commensurate with ... level of autonomy" is the only textual hook for agents. The Act has no agent-specific oversight rule. Whether an agent is high-risk depends on its use case under Annex III or Annex I, not on its being agentic.
- "Automation bias" awareness (14(4)(b)) is a training and interface obligation. Evidence would be training records plus UI design that shows confidence and limitations, not just a log of approvals.

- EUR-Lex HTML for Regulation 2024/1689 did not render through the fetch tool. Quotes are from the OJ mirror, which matches the OJ wording I know, but verify on EUR-Lex before printing.
- Whether a JTC 21 human-oversight deliverable has a project number or enquiry date: not found. One September 2026 newsletter reported that risk management, cybersecurity and logging drafts were rejected at enquiry in summer 2026, while another item in the same newsletter listed logging at enquiry. Unresolved. — [krogrules.com AI Standardisation Watch, Sept 2026](https://krogrules.com/news/edition-16-september-2026) [Search-summary only]

## 2. Deployer obligations and related duties: Articles 26, 4, 27, 86, 50 (plus logs and penalties)

### Takeaway
A deployer of a high-risk system must use it per the instructions, assign oversight to competent, trained and empowered natural persons, monitor it, suspend it and report when it presents a risk, keep its logs for **at least six months**, inform workers' representatives before workplace use, and tell affected people that a high-risk system is used in decisions about them. Public-sector deployers, private providers of public services, and credit-scoring and life/health insurance pricing deployers must also do a fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA) that **describes how human oversight is implemented**. Affected persons get a right to a "clear and meaningful" explanation of the AI's role (Art. 86). AI literacy (Art. 4) has applied since 2 Feb 2025 but was softened by the Omnibus into a "take measures to support" duty. Article 50 transparency applied from 2 Aug 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **26(1)** Deployers "shall take appropriate technical and organisational measures" to use high-risk systems in accordance with the instructions for use. — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(2)** "Deployers shall assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support." — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(3)** These duties are without prejudice to other Union or national law, and deployers remain free to organise their own resources and activities to implement the provider's oversight measures. — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(4)** Where the deployer controls input data, it must ensure the data is "relevant and sufficiently representative in view of the intended purpose". — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(5), monitoring, suspension, incident reporting.** Deployers monitor operation on the basis of the instructions. If use may present a risk, they inform the provider or distributor and the market surveillance authority and "shall suspend the use of that system". For a serious incident they immediately inform the provider, then the importer or distributor and the authorities. **Financial-institution carve-out:** credit institutions meet the monitoring duty by complying with internal-governance rules under Union financial services law. — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(6), logs.** Deployers keep automatically generated logs under their control "for a period appropriate to the intended purpose ... of at least six months, unless provided otherwise in applicable Union or national law". Financial institutions keep them as part of their financial-services documentation. — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(7), workers.** Before putting a high-risk system into service or use at the workplace, employer-deployers "shall inform workers' representatives and the affected workers" that they will be subject to the system, in accordance with Union and national rules and practice on informing workers. — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **26(8)** Public-authority deployers must register their use in the EU database (Art. 49) and must not use an unregistered system. **26(9)** Deployers use the Article 13 information to carry out their GDPR Art. 35 DPIA. **26(10)** sets special rules for post-remote biometric identification in criminal investigations (prior authorisation, no untargeted use, annual reports). **26(11)** Deployers of Annex III systems that make or assist decisions about natural persons "shall inform the natural persons that they are subject to the use of the high-risk AI system". **26(12)** requires cooperation with authorities. — [OJ mirror, Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- **Provider-side log retention.** Providers must keep automatically generated logs under their control for at least six months (Art. 19). Article 12 requires high-risk systems to "technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system". — [Statute, not re-opened] [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Arts. 12 and 19](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **Article 27, FRIA.** It applies to deployers that are bodies governed by public law or private entities providing public services, and to deployers of Annex III points 5(b) (creditworthiness and credit scoring) and 5(c) (life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing); Annex III point 2 (critical infrastructure) is excluded. It is done "prior to deploying" and covers: (a) processes, (b) period and frequency of use, (c) affected categories of persons, (d) specific risks of harm, (e) "a description of the implementation of human oversight measures, according to the instructions for use", and (f) measures if risks materialise, including internal governance and complaint mechanisms. Results are notified to the market surveillance authority on an AI Office template. It may reuse or cross-reference a GDPR DPIA. Paragraphs 4 and 5 carry an "amended" marker on the mirror. The mirror gives application dates of 2 Dec 2027 (Annex III) and 2 Aug 2028 (Annex I). — [OJ mirror, Art. 27](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/27/)
- **Article 86, right to explanation.** "Any affected person" subject to a decision taken by the deployer on the basis of output from an Annex III high-risk system (except point 2), where the decision "produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects that person" in a way they consider adverse to health, safety or fundamental rights, may obtain "clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken". It does not apply where Union or national law provides exceptions, and only where the right "is not otherwise provided for under Union law" (for example under GDPR). No Omnibus marker. — [OJ mirror, Art. 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/)
- **Article 4, AI literacy, as amended by the Omnibus.** The mirror now shows paragraph 1 as "Providers and deployers of AI systems shall take measures to support the development of AI literacy" with "This obligation does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy". A new paragraph 2 says the Commission and Member States "shall support and facilitate the efforts of providers and deployers", and a new paragraph 3 says the Board "shall adopt recommendations, taking into account European competence frameworks". It has applied since 2 Feb 2025. The mirror also still displays the original "to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy" sentence and does not mark clearly which wording is current. — [OJ mirror, Art. 4](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/4/). Commentary agrees that the duty stayed binding (the Commission's proposal would have shifted it to Member States and the Commission) but became effort-based. — [lawandtechnology.eu](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/ai-literacy-digital-omnibus-article-4-ai-act/) [Search-summary only]
- **Article 50, transparency (applied 2 Aug 2026).** Providers must tell people they are interacting with an AI system and must mark synthetic output in a machine-readable way (50(1)–(2)). Deployers must disclose emotion recognition and biometric categorisation (50(3)) and deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text (50(4)). The grace period for generative systems already on the market to meet 50(2) ends 2 Dec 2026. — duties: [Statute, not re-opened] [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); dates: [Cuatrecasas, 30 Jun 2026](https://www.cuatrecasas.com/en/global/intellectual-property/art/council-eu-approves-digital-omnibus-ai) [Secondary] and trends.md §1.3. The Commission consulted on draft Article 50 guidelines until 3 June 2026. — [Agence Europe](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13865/15/european-commission-opens-consultation-on-draft-guidelines-on-ai-transparency-obligations) [Search-summary only]
- **Article 99, penalties.** Breach of Art. 5 prohibitions: up to EUR 35m or 7% of worldwide annual turnover. Breach of listed operator obligations, including deployer obligations under Art. 26 and transparency under Art. 50: up to EUR 15m or 3%. Supplying incorrect or misleading information to authorities: up to EUR 7.5m or 1%. For SMEs, whichever is lower. — [Statute, not re-opened] [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 99](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); tiers confirmed in [regulation-ai.eu](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/penalties/) [Search-summary only]

### Inferences
- For the storyboard, the deployer's minimum evidence pack per high-risk system is: (1) a named roster of overseers with competence, training and authority records (26(2), plus Art. 4 literacy measures); (2) proof the system was used per the instructions (26(1)); (3) a monitoring record and any suspension or report (26(5)); (4) logs kept at least six months, longer where sector law requires (26(6)); (5) proof workers' representatives were informed before go-live (26(7)); (6) notices to affected people (26(11)); (7) a FRIA with its human-oversight section, notified to the authority, where Art. 27 applies; and (8) the ability to give an Art. 86 explanation on request.
- Article 27 appears not to be in the Art. 99(4) fine list (my reading of Art. 99). That would explain why Germany created its own national administrative offence for FRIA breaches (see section 4). Check Art. 99(4) wording on EUR-Lex.
- "At least six months" is a floor. In finance, logs fall under financial-services record-keeping, which typically runs years, so a multinational's real retention is set by the longest applicable sector rule.
- The FRIA trigger list is narrow (public sector, public services, credit, life/health insurance). A multinational bank or insurer is caught via 5(b) and 5(c) regardless of size.

### Gaps
- EUR-Lex text of the Omnibus (Regulation 2026/1744) could not be rendered, so the exact final Article 4 wording, and whether Articles 26, 49 (registration of Art. 6(3) systems), the SME/small mid-cap extension, or a new bias-detection data provision were changed, are **unconfirmed from the primary text**. Commentary on the Commission's November 2025 proposal described those changes, but I could not confirm which survived.
- Whether Article 86 already applies (it sits outside Chapter III, and the original Art. 113 general date was 2 Aug 2026) or follows the deferred high-risk dates: not resolved. In practice it bites only once a system is classed high-risk, and classification duties now start 2 Dec 2027.
- Whether Article 4 breaches carry any EU-level fine: Art. 99 does not appear to list Art. 4. National sanction rules would govern. Not verified.

## 3. Timeline and state of play at 31 October 2026: Omnibus, standards and guidelines

### Takeaway
The Digital Omnibus on AI is **adopted law**: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, published in the OJ on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026. It fixes the high-risk application dates at **2 December 2027 for Annex III** and **2 August 2028 for Annex I**, as fixed dates rather than dates conditional on standards. Prohibitions (since 2 Feb 2025), GPAI obligations (since 2 Aug 2025), Article 50 transparency (since 2 Aug 2026) and Commission GPAI enforcement powers (since 2 Aug 2026) already apply. A new ban on AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery and CSAM, and the end of the Art. 50(2) legacy grace period, both fall on 2 Dec 2026. The **first harmonised standard**, EN 18286 on quality management, was cited on 14 Oct 2026. There is still **no harmonised standard on human oversight**, and the Commission's high-risk classification guidelines remain a **draft** (19 May 2026).

## 4. Member-state divergence: national AI laws, authorities, employment co-determination and sector regulators

### Takeaway
Divergence is real and growing. **Italy** (Law 132/2025, in force 10 Oct 2025) adds sector-specific "human decides" rules for health, the professions, public administration and the judiciary, and an employer duty to inform workers. **Germany** (KI-MIG, in force 29 July 2026) names BNetzA as the default authority and BaFin for the financial sector, and makes failure to give the Art. 86 explanation and failures under Art. 27 (FRIA) national administrative offences. **Spain** has an organic-law bill in Congress (filed 28 May 2026) that its government presents as guaranteeing "human supervision". **France** and the **Netherlands** have not yet enacted their designation laws, so no national AI Act fines are possible there yet. Separately, works-council law in **Germany** (co-determination) and **France** (CSE consultation, enforced by courts suspending AI rollouts) works as a de facto pre-deployment human-oversight gate. Sector supervisors (BaFin, Banca d'Italia/CONSOB/IVASS, EIOPA, EBA) layer their own expectations on top.

Where national law gives a consultation or co-determination right, deploying before that process ends can be stopped by injunction. - Germany makes Art. 86 explanations and FRIAs enforceable nationally with fines (up to EUR 50,000). That suggests explanation-on-request should be built into central tooling now, even though the high-risk dates are deferred.
- Enforcement capacity is uneven: France and the Netherlands could not fine under the AI Act as of autumn 2026, while Germany and Italy have authorities in place.

## 5. GDPR Article 22 and the CJEU (SCHUFA, Dun & Bradstreet Austria): meaningful human intervention and explanation

### Takeaway
GDPR Article 22 already gives people in the EU a right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects, with safeguards of "at least" human intervention, the right to express a view, and the right to contest. **SCHUFA (C-634/21, 7 Dec 2023)** widened "decision" to cover an automated score that a third party draws on strongly. **Dun & Bradstreet Austria (C-203/22, 27 Feb 2025)** held that the right to "meaningful information about the logic involved" means an explanation of the **procedure and principles actually applied**, intelligible enough to let the person contest the decision. Trade secrets do not override this; they are balanced case by case by a court or authority. Unlike the AI Act's high-risk duties, these rules are **in force now** and are being enforced (section 8).

### Cited Findings
- **GDPR Art. 22(1)** gives the right not to be subject to a decision "based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects ... or similarly significantly affects" the person. Exceptions in 22(2): contract necessity, Union or member-state law, explicit consent. **22(3)**: for the contract and consent exceptions, the controller must implement safeguards, "at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision". Art. 15(1)(h) and Arts. 13(2)(f)/14(2)(g) require "meaningful information about the logic involved". — [Statute, not re-opened] [Regulation (EU) 2016/679, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **SCHUFA (C-634/21), 7 December 2023.** The automated establishment of a probability value (credit score) by a credit agency is itself an "automated individual decision" under Art. 22(1) where a third party, to whom it is transmitted, "draws strongly" on it to establish, implement or terminate a contractual relationship. — [Statute/judgment, not re-opened] [CURIA, C-634/21](https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-634/21); commentary consistent: [privacyworld.blog / search summaries](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2026/08/automated-decision-making-lessons-to-be-learned/)
- **Dun & Bradstreet Austria (C-203/22), 27 February 2025.** Art. 15(1)(h) "must be understood as a right to an explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied". The controller must describe them "in such a way that the data subject can understand which of his or her personal data have been used, and how". The explanation must be concise, transparent and intelligible, and it serves the Art. 22(3) right to contest. The facts: a mobile contract was refused after an automated creditworthiness assessment. — [CURIA press release](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/jcms/p1_4811088/en) [Search-summary only]; [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/data-protection/1597626/cjeu-issues-judgment-on-balancing-the-right-of-access-and-protecting-trade-secrets-in-automated-decision-making-processes) [Search-summary only]; [Agence Europe](https://agenceurope.eu/en/bulletin/article/13589/17/a-clear-explanation-must-accompany-any-decision-resulting-from-an-automated-credit-assessment-stresses-court-of-justice-of-european-union) [Search-summary only]
- **D&B on trade secrets.** Commentary reports that the Court held that a controller invoking trade secrets must submit the allegedly protected information to the competent supervisory authority or court, which balances the interests. A national rule cannot exclude access categorically, and disclosure of the algorithm itself is not required. — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/data-protection/1597626/cjeu-issues-judgment-on-balancing-the-right-of-access-and-protecting-trade-secrets-in-automated-decision-making-processes) [Search-summary only; full judgment not opened]
- **Interaction with the AI Act.** AI Act Art. 86 applies only where the explanation right "is not otherwise provided for under Union law", so the GDPR right (as interpreted in D&B) comes first wherever personal data is processed. — [OJ mirror, Art. 86](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/86/)
- **Token human review does not count.** The 2026 Uber decision turned on deactivations taken with no human assessment (section 8). Commentary says a nominal human in the loop does not take a decision out of Art. 22. — [Pearl Cohen](https://www.pearlcohen.com/dutch-data-protection-authority-fines-uber-e825-million-for-fully-automated-deactivation-of-drivers/) [Secondary]; [search summaries on Art. 22 practice](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2026/08/automated-decision-making-lessons-to-be-learned/) [Secondary]

### Inferences
- SCHUFA means a score or recommendation produced by an AI agent can be the "decision" even if a human signs off, where the human in practice follows it. That is exactly the automation-bias risk AI Act Art. 14(4)(b) targets. The two regimes converge: the GDPR asks whether the human involvement is real; the AI Act asks whether the overseer can understand, interpret and override.
- D&B sets the explanation standard that a central oversight function must be able to produce on request today for any automated decision about an EU person: which data, how used, and what principles produced this outcome. It is stronger and earlier than AI Act Art. 86.
- Evidence of "meaningful" human intervention (reviewer identity, time spent, data considered, override rate) is what separates a lawful human-in-the-loop process from a solely automated one.

### Gaps
- Full text of both judgments not opened. Paragraph numbers for the key holdings are not captured.
- The Commission's November 2025 "Digital Omnibus" proposal on data and the GDPR was reported to include amendments to Art. 22 (clarifying when solely automated decisions are permitted for contracts). Its content and legislative status at Oct 2026 were not verified. It is a **proposal**, not adopted law.
- EDPB guidance updated since the 2018 WP251 ADM guidelines: not checked.

## 6. United Kingdom: DUAA ADM reform, ICO, sector-led approach, FCA/PRA, AI Security Institute

### Takeaway
The UK replaced its EU-derived Article 22 with **UK GDPR Articles 22A–22D**, inserted by section 80 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 and **in force since 5 February 2026** (SI 2026/82). Solely automated significant decisions are now generally **permitted** with mandatory safeguards: information, representations, **human intervention**, and contest. They remain restricted where special category data or the new "recognised legitimate interests" basis is involved. "Solely automated" means **no meaningful human involvement**, and the Secretary of State can define what counts by regulation. The ICO's updated ADM guidance was still a consultation draft (closed 29 May 2026) with no final version confirmed. There is **no UK AI bill**: the AI Security Institute has no statutory powers, and regulation stays sector-led. The FCA does not plan AI-specific rules, and its July 2026 Mills Review pushes toward live monitoring rather than deploy-time validation.

### Cited Findings
- **Commencement.** "The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (Commencement No. 6 and Transitional and Saving Provisions) Regulations 2026", SI 2026/82 (C. 10), made 29 Jan 2026. Reg. 2 brings section 80 (ADM) and Schedule 6, among others, into force on **5 February 2026**. Reg. 5 preserves the old safeguards for decisions taken before that date. Reg. 3 brings section 103 and Schedule 10 (data-subject complaints) into force on 19 June 2026. — [legislation.gov.uk, SI 2026/82](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/made) [Primary]; s.80 commencement note: [legislation.gov.uk, DUAA s.80](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/80) [Primary]. - **Art. 22A.** A decision is based solely on automated processing where "there is no meaningful human involvement in the taking of the decision". - **Art. 22B (restrictions).** A significant decision based wholly or partly on special category data may not be solely automated unless "the decision is based entirely on processing of personal data to which the data subject has given explicit consent", or it is necessary for a contract or required or authorised by law and Art. 9(2)(g) (substantial public interest) applies. - **FCA.** No AI-specific regulation planned; it relies on existing principles-based rules, including the Consumer Duty and the Senior Managers and Certification Regime. — [Covington Global Policy Watch, Apr 2026](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/04/uk-financial-services-regulators-approach-to-artificial-intelligence-in-2026/) [Search-summary only]. - **PRA SS1/23** "Model risk management principles for banks": five principles (model identification and risk classification; governance; development and implementation; independent validation; risk mitigants). It took effect 17 May 2024 and expressly covers AI/ML models. — [Bank of England (not opened)](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/prudential-regulation/publication/2023/may/model-risk-management-principles-for-banks-ss); details from [CIMCON](https://cimcon.com/understanding-ss1-23-principles-for-model-risk-management-mrm/) [Search-summary only, vendor]

### Inferences
- The UK and EU now point in opposite directions on solely automated decisions. The EU default is a prohibition with exceptions (GDPR Art. 22); the UK default is permission with safeguards (Art. 22C), restricted for special category data and recognised legitimate interests. Both converge on the same operational requirement: a human who can actually intervene, plus a channel for representations and contest. A central oversight console can serve both by providing the same four capabilities: notice, representations, human intervention and contest.
- "Meaningful human involvement" (UK Art. 22A) is the UK counterpart of SCHUFA and Uber in the EU. If the human cannot change the outcome, the decision is solely automated and the 22C safeguards apply.
- The FCA's live-monitoring direction (Mills) and the US SR 26-2 exclusion of agentic AI (trends.md §5.2) make the same point: supervisors are moving away from one-off validation, but no UK rule yet prescribes continuous oversight of agents.

### Gaps
- Final ICO ADM guidance: not confirmed published as of 31 Oct 2026. Check ico.org.uk.
- Any Secretary of State regulations under Art. 22D defining meaningful human involvement: none found.
- A reported machinery-of-government change (DSIT dissolved 20 July 2026, AI policy and AISI moved to the Cabinet Office) appears in one search result only. Do not use it without verification.
- PRA SS1/23's exact scope wording (it applies in full to banks with internal-model approval; others proportionately) was not read on the Bank of England site.
- No UK enforcement action on ADM or AI oversight was found.

## 7. Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (CETS 225)

### Takeaway
The Convention opened for signature on 5 Sept 2024. The **EU became its first Party** after Parliament consented on 11 March 2026 (455 to 101, 74 abstentions), the Council adopted Decision (EU) 2026/1080 on 21 April 2026, and the EU deposited its instrument on **15 May 2026**. The treaty is **not yet in force**: it needs five ratifications, at least three by Council of Europe member states. Reported signatories number about 21, including the UK, US, Canada, Norway, Switzerland, Japan and Israel. None of the non-EU signatories had ratified per the sources found.

### Cited Findings
- **EU conclusion steps.** Parliament consent 11 March 2026 (455 for, 101 against, 74 abstentions). — [OEIL document summary](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1891784); [Council of Europe news](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/-/eu-parliament-backs-eu-conclusion-of-the-council-of-europe-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence) [Search-summary only]. Council Decision (EU) 2026/1080 of 21 April 2026 "on the conclusion, on behalf of the European Union". The Convention text is in OJ L as 2026/1081 of 13 May 2026. — [alhukuk.com reproduction of title](https://alhukuk.com/en/news/1813); [nicfab.eu](https://www.nicfab.eu/en/posts/cets225-ratifica-ue/) [Search-summary only]
- **EU deposit.** The EU deposited its instrument on 15 May 2026, at the 135th session of the Committee of Ministers in Chișinău, "becoming the first Party to have ratified the treaty". — [lawandtechnology.eu](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/council-of-europe-framework-convention-artificial-intelligence-eu-ratification-en/) [Search-summary only]
- **Status at mid-October 2026.** 21 signatories and one Party (the EU) as of 13 Oct 2026; not in force. — [search summary of tracker sources incl. regulatorik.ai](https://regulatorik.ai/en/framework/coe-ai) [Search-summary only]. Two sites claiming entry into force on 1 Nov 2025 are inconsistent with the EU being the first Party in May 2026 and should be disregarded. — [theworldofai.org](https://theworldofai.org/ai-compliance/coe-framework-convention/) (unreliable)
- **Entry-into-force rule (Art. 30).** Five signatories must consent, including at least three Council of Europe member states; entry into force is the first day of the month after three months from the fifth consent. The EU counts toward the five but not the three. — [Convention text, rm.coe.int](https://rm.coe.int/1680afae3c) [Statute, not re-opened]; [lawandtechnology.eu](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/council-of-europe-framework-convention-artificial-intelligence-eu-ratification-en/) [Search-summary only]
- **Oversight-relevant obligations** (once in force for a Party): measures to ensure "adequate transparency and oversight requirements tailored to the specific contexts and risks" (Art. 8); accountability (Art. 9); remedies, including documentation available to affected persons (Art. 14); procedural safeguards where AI significantly affects human rights, including notice of interacting with an AI system (Art. 15); risk and impact management (Art. 16). — [Convention text](https://rm.coe.int/1680afae3c) [Statute, not re-opened]
- The Treaty Office chart returned HTTP 403. — [coe.int chart](https://www.coe.int/en/web/conventions/full-list?module=signatures-by-treaty&treatynum=225)

### Inferences
- For an EU multinational the Convention adds little directly: the EU implements it through the AI Act. Its keynote value is that the UK, US and Canada signed the same oversight and transparency principles, which gives a common reference across jurisdictions that otherwise diverge.

### Gaps
- Exact signature dates for Canada, Japan and others, and whether any Council of Europe member state ratified after mid-October 2026: not verified (the Treaty Office page was blocked).

## 8. Penalties and enforcement actions to date relevant to human oversight and automated decisions

### Takeaway
No AI Act fine has been verified anywhere as of early October 2026, and several states (France, the Netherlands) still lack designated authorities. The decisive enforcement on human oversight is under the **GDPR**. On **21 August 2026** the Dutch AP fined **Uber EUR 824.99 million** for fully automated deactivation of drivers with no human assessment, plus insufficient information. It is the second-highest GDPR fine ever, followed a complaint by 171 French drivers via the CNIL, and Uber has appealed. French courts have **suspended AI rollouts** for failure to consult works councils. In the UK no ADM enforcement action was found.

### Cited Findings
- **AP v Uber (2026).** Fine of EUR 824,990,000 imposed 21 Aug 2026 for prohibited fully automated decision-making (software flagged suspected fraud or low ratings, and deactivations followed automatically with no human assessment) and insufficient information to drivers. Period covered: 2018–2022. The case originated in a Ligue des droits de l'Homme complaint to the CNIL on behalf of 171 French drivers; the AP acted as lead authority in cooperation with the CNIL. AP deputy chair Monique Verdier: "A computer should not make decisions on its own that have major consequences for you." — [Pearl Cohen, 31 Aug 2026](https://www.pearlcohen.com/dutch-data-protection-authority-fines-uber-e825-million-for-fully-automated-deactivation-of-drivers/) [Secondary]; [Dutch IT Channel, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.dutchitchannel.nl/news/758232/ap-heeft-uber-een-boete-van-bijna-825-miljoen-euro) [Search-summary only]; [Squire Patton Boggs Privacy World, 26 Aug 2026](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2026/08/automated-decision-making-lessons-to-be-learned/) [Secondary: describes the same decision without naming Uber; fine calculated on worldwide turnover; subject to appeal]. Uber has appealed and says the blocks were not final. — [ioplus.nl](https://ioplus.nl/nl/posts/uber-beboet-met-825-miljoen--wat-het-echt-betekent) [Search-summary only]
- **Earlier AP fines on Uber.** EUR 290m (2024) for transfers of driver data to the US; EUR 10m (2023, published 2024) for transparency and access-rights failings. Both are contested by Uber. — [AP, transfers decision](https://www.autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/documenten/boete-uber-doorgifte-naar-vs); [AP, privacy-rights decision](https://www.autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl/documenten/boete-uber-privacyrechten) [Primary, seen in search]. The AP's own page for the 2026 decision was not found by site search, so the 2026 figures rest on law-firm and news reports.
- **Italy, Garante v Foodinho/Glovo.** EUR 5m fine (2024) for algorithmic management of delivery riders. — [search summary](https://luxgap.com/articles/uber-sanction-article-22-rgpd-decisions-automatisees-2026/?lang=en) [Search-summary only; verify on garanteprivacy.it]
- **No AI Act fines yet.** A tracker dated 2 Oct 2026 found no verified AI Act fine; a 29 Sept 2026 guide says neither the Commission nor any national authority has published one. A claim of EUR 85m in AI Office fines in March 2026 is uncorroborated and inconsistent and should be disregarded. — [Vorplabs EU enforcement tracker](https://vorplabs.com/ai-regulatory-updates/eu-enforcement) [Search-summary only]; [informedclearly.com](https://informedclearly.com/en/ai/52202/eu-ai-act-first-fines-enforcement-2026) (unreliable)
- **Penalty ceilings.** AI Act Art. 99: EUR 35m/7% (prohibitions), EUR 15m/3% (operator obligations including deployer Art. 26 and transparency Art. 50), EUR 7.5m/1% (misleading information). GDPR Art. 83(5): up to EUR 20m or 4% for breaches including Art. 22. Germany's KI-MIG: up to EUR 50,000 for its national offences (Art. 86 explanation, Art. 27 FRIA and others). — [Statute, not re-opened] [AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj); [KI-MIG § 15(3)](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ki-mig/BJNR0DF0B0026.html) [Primary]

- **Collective action.** In 2026 the WIE International foundation filed a Dutch collective action (WAMCA) against Uber over algorithmic handling of driver data. — [rechtspraak.nl filing](https://www.rechtspraak.nl/binaries/_rts_1788360682396/content/assets/wamca/2026/stichting-wie-international-v-uber-bv-en-uber-technologies-inc.pdf) [Search-summary only]

### Inferences
- The strongest keynote fact on "why oversight must be real" is the Uber decision: EUR 825m for automated outcomes with no human assessment, under a law already in force, while the AI Act's high-risk oversight rules are still 13 months away. Note that it is under appeal.
- Enforcement is coming from three directions at once: data-protection authorities (GDPR Art. 22), labour courts and works councils (pre-deployment consultation), and later AI Act market-surveillance authorities. A central oversight function needs evidence that satisfies all three: a record of human review per decision, a record of consultation per deployment, and a record of oversight assignment per system.

- Garante Foodinho figures and the date were not verified on the Garante's site.
- UK: no ICO ADM enforcement found. No FCA enforcement relating to AI oversight found.
- No CNIL AI-specific sanction since the Uber cooperation was researched.
