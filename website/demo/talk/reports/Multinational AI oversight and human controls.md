# Centralize the library, localize the human

A multinational operating in about ten countries runs AI oversight centrally by owning one control library, one taxonomy of oversight patterns, one inventory of systems and agents, and the thresholds that send a case upward. The reviewer, the pre-deployment gates and the raw evidence stay in each country. Firms that publish their set-up describe this hub-and-spoke arrangement, and regulators now accept it in writing: Singapore lets a local financial institution rely on its group's AI framework, and the EU AI Act lets financial institutions meet its monitoring and log duties through their existing governance. Countries differ less on the human they want than on everything around that human. California, Colorado, Quebec, the UK, Canada's Bill C-36 and Brazil's PL 2338 all describe a reviewer who understands the output and can change the decision. They split on whether the review must be human at all (Brazil's LGPD: no), whether solely automated decisions are banned or allowed with safeguards (EU versus UK), when the duties start (now in China, Korea and Vietnam; December 2027 for the EU AI Act's Annex III), where evidence may be viewed (China counts remote viewing as an export), and how long it must be kept (six months to ten years). The established pattern controls (action approval, decision review, contest and appeal, change authorization, monitoring and halt) fit the akka/human project's five flavors and fourteen patterns almost completely. The emerging patterns come with agents and move the human up a level, to approving a policy, a plan, a mandate or an agent's tier of authority, and to measuring whether reviewers are still paying attention. Today's enforcement comes from data-protection law and labour courts, not the AI Act: the Dutch regulator fined Uber €825 million for automated deactivations with no human assessment, and no AI Act fine has been verified. In the product today, the console has the vocabulary and a per-system answer: controls that apply by jurisdiction, five human flavors in `human_config`, verdicts, and a run-time response that can be narrowed to one country. The akka/human prototype adds the fourteen patterns, competence checks, independence rules and oversight-quality figures. Neither one records jurisdiction on a decision, a verdict or a reviewer. Neither combines a central policy with local settings, rolls verdicts up across systems, or keeps evidence in its region. The next storyboard can add the organization and the patterns truthfully if it presents country-scoped verdicts and organization-wide views as the next step, not as features that exist today.

## Ten regimes describe the same reviewer and split on everything around it

**The law has converged on one definition of a meaningful human reviewer.** California's ADMT regulations say a decision is not substantially automated only if a reviewer "knows how to interpret and use" the output, reviews it with other relevant information, and has "the authority to make or change the decision", with compliance due **1 January 2027** ([CPPA approved text](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/ccpa_updates_cyber_risk_admt_appr_text.pdf)). Colorado's SB 26-189 (signed 14 May 2026, effective 1 January 2027) gives a right to "human review and reconsideration". The reviewer must be able to approve, modify or override, must consider primary evidence, must be trained, and must "not simply defer to the automated output" ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/05/colorado-ai-act-repeal-new-transparency-law)). Quebec's section 12.1, in force since September 2023, requires that the person can submit observations to "a member of their personnel who is able to review the decision" ([IAB Canada guide](https://iabcanada.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/IAB-Canada_BPG-AI-Disclosures_June-2022.pdf); primary text blocked, confirm before quoting). Canada's Bill C-36, tabled 15 June 2026, would extend that model federally ([Library of Parliament](https://lop.parl.ca/staticfiles/PublicWebsite/Home/ResearchPublications/LegislativeSummaries/PDF/45-1/PV_45-1-C36-E.pdf)). The UK's new Article 22A, in force since **5 February 2026**, treats a decision as solely automated where there is "no meaningful human involvement" ([legislation.gov.uk, DUAA s.80](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/80); [SI 2026/82](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/made)). The CJEU's SCHUFA ruling makes the same point from the other side: when a lender "draws strongly" on an automated score, producing the score is itself the automated decision, so a nominal human signature does not take it out of scope ([CURIA, C-634/21](https://curia.europa.eu/juris/liste.jsf?num=C-634/21)). For a central oversight function, one human-review control with one reviewer record can therefore meet the core requirement in most of the markets in this report.

**The divergence is in the mechanics, and it is large.** The EU starts from a prohibition on solely automated significant decisions, with exceptions (GDPR Art. 22). The UK now starts from permission with mandatory safeguards: information, representations, human intervention and contest (Art. 22C). Brazil's data-protection law gives a right to review, but Congress upheld the 2019 veto of the clause requiring a natural person, "so the review can be done by another machine" ([Teletime, 2 Oct 2019](https://teletime.com.br/02/10/2019/congresso-mantem-veto-e-lgpd-fica-sem-revisao-humana-de-decisoes-automatizadas/)). Korea and China give the affected person a right to refuse a fully automated decision ([Prighter, PIPA Art. 37-2](https://prighter.com/resources/laws/korean-pipa/main/articles/article-37-2); [DigiChina, PIPL Art. 24](https://digichina.stanford.edu/work/translation-personal-information-protection-law-of-the-peoples-republic-of-china-effective-nov-1-2021/)). Some sectors already require that a person make the decision. Italy's Law 132/2025 reserves the decision to the doctor, the professional, the public official and the magistrate ([Normattiva](https://www.normattiva.it/uri-res/N2Ls?urn:nir:stato:legge:2025-09-23;132); [unofficial translation](https://ai-resources.eu/atti-normativi/italia/legge-132-2025/)). At least eight US states bar health insurers from relying solely on AI for adverse decisions ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/states-continue-efforts-to-regulate-ai-in-healthcare)). California's SB 947 requires human corroboration before discipline or termination from **1 July 2027** ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/10/california-enacts-new-laws-governing-employer-use-of-ai)). The duties also start at different times. The EU AI Act's Article 14 applies from **2 December 2027** for Annex III systems and **2 August 2028** for Annex I systems ([AI Act Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)). Korea's Framework Act has bound high-impact operators since 22 January 2026, with fines deferred for at least a year ([US Commercial Service](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act)). Vietnam's AI Law, implementing decree and list of 46 high-risk systems all took effect between March and August 2026 ([Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/vietnam-takes-major-step-in-ai-governance-with-new-guiding-decree/)).

The table shows at least six distinct human-control requirements for one AI output. Three of them (a human who may be a machine, a refusal right, and a reviewer who must sit with the data) do not appear in the keynote at all. The remaining markets add contrast rather than new requirements. Japan regulates through voluntary guidelines with no penalties ([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/japans-first-ai-legislation-becomes-law-focus-promoting-research-and-development-no)). India has no AI statute but requires payment data to be stored only in India ([RBI FAQ](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/FAQView.aspx?Id=130)). Australia dropped its mandatory guardrails but will require automated-decision disclosure in privacy policies from **10 December 2026** ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)).

**Enforcement is coming through data-protection authorities and courts, not the AI Act.** On **21 August 2026** the Dutch data-protection authority fined Uber **€824.99 million** because drivers were deactivated automatically, with no human assessment. It is the second-highest GDPR fine ever, and Uber has appealed ([Pearl Cohen](https://www.pearlcohen.com/dutch-data-protection-authority-fines-uber-e825-million-for-fully-automated-deactivation-of-drivers/)). An October 2026 tracker found no verified AI Act fine ([Vorplabs](https://vorplabs.com/ai-regulatory-updates/eu-enforcement), search summary). France and the Netherlands have not yet designated the authorities that would issue one. Courts in Nanterre and Créteil have suspended AI rollouts until works councils were consulted, and one ruling held that even a pilot needs consultation ([CMS](https://cms.law/fr/fra/legal-updates/l-ia-suspendue-le-juge-exige-la-consultation-du-cse-avant-tout-deploiement)). In the Americas, enforcement that actually demands human review is thin. A stipulated order bars enforcement of Colorado's law until after the court rules on xAI's challenge, which waits on the final rules ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-ai-law-in-flux-comprehensive-replacement-bill-signed-after-federal-court-blocks-predecessors-enforcement/)). Texas opened a TRAIGA complaint portal on 1 September 2026 and no action has been reported ([CSA research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-texas-traiga-enforcement-portal-20260913-c/)). So a central function needs three kinds of evidence at once: a human-review record for each decision (for data-protection authorities), a consultation record for each deployment (for labour courts), and an oversight assignment for each system (for future AI Act authorities).

## Multinationals centralize the library and localize the person who decides

### Published programmes run a hub with spokes, tiered by materiality

The companies that describe their set-up all use a hybrid. HSBC built a Group AI Review Committee and then "embedded AI Review Councils across the organisation" ([HSBC](https://www.hsbc.com/who-we-are/hsbc-and-digital/hsbc-and-ai/transforming-hsbc-with-ai), search summary). Standard Chartered operates in 41 markets with a Responsible AI Council that approves models and a separate AI Safety Council that owns the inventory ([The Digital Banker](https://thedigitalbanker.com/making-ai-adoption-safe-how-standard-chartered-is-putting-humans-in-control-of-ai/), trade press). IBM's central AI Ethics Board relies on business-unit "focal points" that triage low-risk cases and send higher-risk ones up ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/a-look-into-ibms-ai-ethics-governance-framework)). An independent audit of AstraZeneca, the one in-depth outside account, recommends harmonising compliance assurance across business areas while adapting risk assurance locally ([Mökander & Floridi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9152664/)). That is the best one-line statement of the operating model in the sources. What sends work to the hub is materiality, not a strictest-everywhere rule. In MAS's 2024 thematic review, most Singapore banks applied baseline standards to all AI and enhanced standards to higher-materiality uses, and required independent validation only for those higher-materiality uses ([Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/05/good-practices-for-ai-model-risk-management-for-singapore-financ.html), search summary). No published firm disclosure describes a "strictest common denominator" AI policy. The idea appears in practitioner FAQs, not in company accounts.

Regulators have started to write this structure into their rules. MAS's final Guidelines let a Singapore institution in a global group "take guidance from or leverage their group's AI risk management frameworks" if they meet the local expectations ([Allen & Gledhill](https://www.allenandgledhill.com/sg/publication/articles/34686/mas-publishes-ai-risk-management-guidelines-setting-out-supervisory-expectations-on-responsible-ai-adoption)). The EU AI Act deems a financial institution's existing internal-governance rules to meet its quality-management, monitoring and log-keeping duties ([Art. 17](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/17/); [Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)). This is the only text found in which one evidence record formally serves two regimes. It is limited to the EU and to financial institutions, and no regulator anywhere has said that one record satisfies several countries.

Conflicting laws are not the failure mode the sources document. The documented failure is central principles that reach the units unevenly. AstraZeneca took over six months to write its principles and another year to embed them, some units were unaware of the compliance document during the audit, and business areas "understood risk differently" ([Mökander & Floridi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9152664/)). In the Bank of England and FCA survey, most UK firms reported "three or more accountable persons or bodies" for AI. The same survey found 46% of firms with only partial understanding of the AI they use, and a third of use cases running on third-party models ([BoE/FCA 2024](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/report/2024/artificial-intelligence-in-uk-financial-services-2024)). Maturity surveys use incompatible definitions but point the same way. Seventy-seven percent of organisations are "working on" AI governance ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/resources/article/ai-governance-profession-report)), while only 21% report a mature governance model for agentic AI ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/emerging-technologies/ai-agents-scaling-faster.html), search summary). The deck rule against naming banks or customers applies to every firm in this paragraph. On slides, describe them by structure ("a bank in 41 markets"), not by name.

### Data, labour law, representatives and clocks pull work back into each country

Four kinds of rule force parts of central oversight back into each country. **Data location** comes first. China counts a foreign reviewer's remote viewing of data stored in China as an outbound transfer ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/key-questions-answered-chinas-regulation-of-international-data-transfers-effective-1-september-2022)). Without official approval, no data stored in China may go to a foreign court or enforcement body ([Cooley](https://cdp.cooley.com/part-3-pipls-localization-requirements-and-restrictions-on-responding-to-foreign-judicial-and-enforcement-agencies/)). The CAC's October 2025 FAQ softens one case: overseas staff who access the data while physically in mainland China do not trigger a transfer ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/11/china-issues-clarifications-cross-border-data-transfer-rules), search summary). India requires payment data processed abroad to return within one business day or 24 hours ([RBI FAQ](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/FAQView.aspx?Id=130)). Vietnam now treats processing locally collected data on a foreign platform as a transfer, which needs an impact assessment refreshed every six months ([Baker McKenzie](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/vietnams-pdpl-in-focus-what-to-know-and-watch-for/), search summary). **Local accountable persons** come second. Korea requires a domestic agent from foreign operators above any of three thresholds: KRW 1 trillion in revenue, KRW 10 billion in AI revenue, or a million daily Korean users ([US Commercial Service](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act)). The EU's authorised representative keeps documentation for **ten years** ([Art. 22](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/22/)). Dubai's DIFC allows high-risk autonomous processing only under one of three conditions, one of which is appointing an Autonomous Systems Officer ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/de/insights/publications/2026/01/ai-regulation-in-the-difc-personal-data-processed-through-autonomous-and-semi-autonomous-systems)). **Labour law** comes third. French works councils must be consulted before AI rollouts, and courts enforce this by suspending deployments. German works councils co-determine any technical system able to monitor employees, and must be informed and consulted when AI is used in planned work processes ([BetrVG § 87](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/betrvg/__87.html); [§ 90](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/betrvg/__90.html)). The trigger is the effect on working conditions, not the technology: a Paris order of 10 February 2026 held that Copilot 365 did not require consultation ([CMS](https://cms.law/fr/fra/legal-updates/deploiement-de-l-ia-quand-les-effets-comptent-plus-que-la-technologie), search summary). **Clocks and retention** come fourth. Vietnam wants incident reports within 72 hours for urgent incidents and five working days for other serious ones. Colorado wants a post-adverse notice within 30 days. Retention ranges from six months for EU deployer logs and Chinese labelling logs ([CAC](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/14/c_1743654684782215.htm)), through three years in Colorado, four years for California employment-decision records ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/zh-hans/insights/publications/2025/08/california-adopts-new-employment-ai-regulations-effective-october-1-2025)) and five years for Korea's draft oversight records ([Baker Botts](https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102low8/south-korean-ministry-of-science-and-ict-issues-package-of-regulations-to-supplem)), to ten years for EU authorised representatives.

The law does not require the reviewer to sit in any particular country. The EU asks only that oversight go to natural persons with "competence, training and authority" ([Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)). Locality comes from data access and labour law. No regulator or firm source was found on follow-the-sun review queues or on reviewers' language. Plain-language requirements exist for explanations to affected people (Italy, Brazil's PL 2338 and Canada's C-36), not for reviewers. Treat claims about time-zone staffing or reviewer language as design choices, not legal requirements.

### Evidence stays in-country while verdicts travel

The researchers draw the same design from these constraints. It is their synthesis, not a stated rule. Raw prompts, outputs and decision logs that contain personal or payment data stay in the country and are reviewed by local staff. The central function receives a record without personal data: the decision, the control result, the reviewer and role, the time, and a pointer to the evidence. Retention becomes an attribute of each record, set to the longest period that applies, rather than one global setting. The rollout calendar gains a per-country gate saying the consultation is complete, and in France that gate applies to pilots too. Spokes decide individual cases. The hub sets thresholds, approves high-risk cases, and owns the library and the inventory. This is the operating model the keynote can draw. In the current product, the hub half already exists and the spoke half does not (see "The product has the human mechanics but not the country dimension" below).

## Fourteen patterns cover the law, and agents move approval up to the policy

### Three generations of vocabulary, one functional test

Oversight vocabulary comes in three layers. The loop metaphors came first: in, on and over the loop, plus human-in-command ([HLEG Ethics Guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/ethics-guidelines-trustworthy-ai); [Singapore Model Framework 2020](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/singapore-model-ai-governance-framework-from-principles-to-governance)). Next came "meaningful human control" from weapons ethics, with its two conditions: the system tracks human reasons, and outcomes can be traced to a responsible human ([Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00015/full)). The newest layer is the EU AI Act's functional list. It names no loop model. It says the overseer must be able to understand and monitor the system, stay aware of automation bias, interpret the output, disregard, override or reverse it, and stop the system "in a safe state". For remote biometric identification it adds the Act's only rule that counts people: two people must separately confirm a match ([Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/)). "On the loop" means design-time intervention plus monitoring in the HLEG's usage and run-time supervision in industry glossaries, so the deck should define it once or avoid it. No harmonised standard on human oversight exists yet. The relevant CEN-CENELEC deliverable is a draft whose numbering sources report inconsistently ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/10/First-Harmonised-Technical-Standard-Published-Under-the-EU-AI-Act); [Genorma](https://genorma.com/en/standards/pren-18229-1)).

The akka/human taxonomy works with this functional list rather than against it. A **flavor** says who decides and how tightly the person is tied to the system: system authorization, domain decision, feedback, monitoring and halt. A **pattern** says what situation they decide; there are fourteen. A control declares one of each, and the engine refuses a pattern that does not run under its flavor (`human/docs/01-patterns.md`; `human/java/human-engine/.../Settings.java`). The mapping is many-to-many. For example, action approval runs under both system authorization and domain decision. The keynote and its supporting notes sometimes call the five "families", and the storyboard should use the project's term, flavor. Each of the Act's capabilities maps to a flavor. Understanding, monitoring and awareness of automation bias map to monitoring and oversight quality. Interpreting maps to domain decision. Override and reversal map to system authorization and domain decision. Stopping maps to halt. The two-person rule is a dial on match and adjudication.

Four things stand out. First, **contest and appeal is the most widely binding pattern** (at least seven regimes in force), and decision review is second, through sector rules. Second, **action approval, the pattern agent products use most, has no binding legal anchor for private deployments**. It rests on guidance and protocol "SHOULDs". The keynote can say this plainly. Third, the corpus behind the console weights human controls the opposite way from agent practice. Of its 38 human controls, 19 are domain decisions and 10 are halts, but only 2 are system authorization (`demo/talk/research_notes/.../keynote_and_governance_model.md` §3). Fourth, Korea's draft definition of "human management and supervision" lists "defined intervention points, interruption and rollback mechanisms, inspection schedules, and staff training" ([Baker Botts](https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102low8/south-korean-ministry-of-science-and-ict-issues-package-of-regulations-to-supplem)). That one sentence covers four akka/human concepts: action approval or decision review, halt and restart, scheduled attestation, and overseer competence. If the final notice keeps that wording, it is the closest single legal text to the project's model.

### Agents move the human from the instance to the policy

The agent-era patterns share one shift: the human's act moves out of the individual decision and into setting its bounds. **Machine approvers** are the clearest case. In Claude Code's auto mode, a two-stage classifier reviews each action before it runs, with a 0.4% false-positive rate on 10,000 real actions, a 17% false-negative rate on 52 real overeager actions, and escalation to the human after three consecutive or twenty total denials ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)). The human now authorizes the policy and handles the exceptions. **Plan-level approval** moves review from each step to the strategy ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/our-framework-for-developing-safe-and-trustworthy-agents)). **Delegated mandates** set the envelope in advance: Google's AP2 has the user sign an intent mandate with price and timing limits for purchases made while they are absent ([Descope](https://www.descope.com/learn/post/ap2)). Singapore's v1.5 framework prefers "deterministic, structurally-enforced limits" for higher-risk actions ([Inside Global Tech](https://www.insideglobaltech.com/2026/06/18/singapore-updates-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai/)). **Tiered decision authority** appears in China's agent Opinions, which require developers to classify each decision as the user's alone, needing user authorization, or autonomous, and give users "final decision-making power" over what the agent decides ([RITS](https://rits.shanghai.nyu.edu/ai/china-issues-first-national-policy-framework-dedicated-to-ai-agents)). The three tiers correspond directly to domain decision, action approval and a delegated mandate. **Agent registries** are becoming a supervisory object. The HKMA's 15 October 2026 ART framework announced a discussion paper proposing "a registry of material A.I. agents" and "effective intervention mechanisms" ([HKMA](https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/news-and-media/press-releases/2026/10/20261015-3/)). Hong Kong's privacy commissioner recommends an agent register recording autonomy level, data access and plugins ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/hong-kong-s-new-guidance-on-protecting-personal-data-privacy-in-agentic-ai-use/)). The DIFC requires a register of system use cases, APRA an inventory of AI tools and "controls over agentic and autonomous workflows", and MAS an inventory "at an appropriate level of granularity". **Oversight-quality telemetry**, meaning measurements of the reviewers rather than the AI, now appears in government guidance through Singapore's override-rate and response-time metrics. Across Singapore, OWASP, Google and Anthropic the order is the same: deterministic bounds first, monitoring second, and human approval last, reserved for irreversible or high-consequence actions. That reverses the older assumption that the human is the primary safeguard.

Four kinds of control fit poorly as raised-and-decided events, because the human acts outside the run: delegated mandates and budgets, the affected person's consent, opt-out or refusal, sampled QA review, and institutional review before adoption. The researchers suggest two ways to model them: as preconditions and evidence attached to the five flavors, or as a sixth group called "affected-person rights", separate from the overseer's patterns. Several modifiers apply to every pattern and are better shown as settings than as patterns: how many people and how independent they are, the overseer's competence and authority, whether the approver is a person, a classifier or a policy, whether the work waits, and what evidence is kept.

### Oversight that cannot show it works is losing legal weight

The research is consistent: adding a person does not by itself make decisions better. A meta-analysis of 106 experiments found that human-AI combinations performed **worse than the better of the two alone** (Hedges' g = −0.23), with the losses concentrated in decision tasks ([Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/)). Across 41 government policies requiring human oversight, Green found that people could not perform the oversight expected of them, and that the policies gave "a false sense of security" ([Green](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05067)). When Kentucky adopted a pretrial risk tool, following it would have raised releases by 37 points; judges' discretion held the actual rise to 4 ([Stevenson & Doleac](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejpol/v16y2024i4p382-414.html)). Developers accept **93%** of Claude Code's permission prompts, and approval fatigue follows ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode)). Regulators are responding by moving from "a human is present" to "the human is able, informed and authorised" (AI Act, CPPA, EDPB) and now to "show that the oversight works". Singapore names override rates and response times, Canada's directive requires verified training at the highest impact level, and the Uber fine shows what happens to oversight that exists only on paper. Cognitive forcing, which asks for the person's judgment before showing the AI's, reduces over-reliance more than explanations do, although users like it less ([Buçinca et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692)).

This is where akka/human differs most from simple approval queues. It enforces a minimum review time, which the decision is refused for or recorded as breaching. It computes review times, approval and override rates, and agreement with the AI per reviewer and per week on the server. And it has a full pattern, oversight quality, that sends a signal to the control's accountable owner when, for example, more than 20% of a week's approvals fall under the minimum review time (`human/spec/fixtures/controls-patterns.yaml`, pt-0014; `human/java/human-engine/.../Figures.java`). Its verdicts judge the oversight, not the AI: "A reviewer who denies an unsafe action has made the control work." Two limits apply. The project's registry says oversight quality "can block new high-risk decisions", but the study found no code that blocks. And the project's two-person restart after a halt goes beyond every law reviewed, so the keynote must present it as good practice, never as a legal requirement.

## The product has the human mechanics but not the country dimension

Internal sources in this section are repository paths. `human/…` is `C:\Users\tyler\human`, and other paths are relative to `C:\Users\tyler\ai-console`. Three states need to be kept apart: built in the Akka local console, built only in the akka/human prototype (version 0.1.0-SNAPSHOT, in a separate repository, which no ai-console document mentions), and designed or not started.

**The console handles jurisdiction per system, and does it well.** The pinned corpus holds 241 frameworks across 38 jurisdictions and 1,718 controls. Each control's applicability is a set of conditions on survey facts, such as `jurisdictions includes_any [eu]` for AI Act Art. 14(4)(e) (`samples/loan-origination/governance/corpus/2026-10/`). The Loan Origination survey answers eight jurisdictions. The system's bundle holds the union of every applicable control, and each control applies wherever the system runs. Inside a system, nothing is scoped to a request's or a deployment's country. Verdicts name their control, environment, time and conclusion, but carry **no jurisdiction or region**. Compliance is asked per system, and its "every environment" means lifecycle stages, not countries (`docs/plans/compliance.md`; `docs/plans/verdicts.md`). No view rolls compliance up across systems. Region does exist at run time, outside governance: entity replication filters, gateway header routes (the "DE route" in scene 26) and Optimize experiment segments on `x-region`, which scene 27 uses to send only Germany back to the original model. For central policy, a company baseline is built, but a system pins only one and nothing combines several. Corporate releases with non-waivable mandates are designed but not built. Layered overlays are explicitly rejected (ADR-010, `docs/plans/corpus-lifecycle/plan.md` §3.4). The only built "central floor that teams may only tighten" is the organization policy for exit conditions (`.akka/governance/policy.yaml`). Exit conditions are a separate concept, the build's definition of done, and must never be presented as controls or verdicts. On the human side, the console knows the five flavors, the modes HITL, HOTL and HALT, and the full `human_config` fields. Its Human page has Waiting, Answered, Standing, Halts and Officers tabs, built on simulated trace-ledger history, and it shows domain decisions read-only. The console's Agents directory lists Akka agent components and the non-Akka agents seen at the gateway, with their type (including "autonomous") and tools. It has no owner or jurisdiction field (`kalix-console/apps/local-console/src/features/agents/schemas.ts`).

**The akka/human prototype has most of the human mechanics the research asks for.** It runs all fourteen patterns on one engine and one HTTP contract. Three services pass its conformance suite. Its companion on port 9167 merges the queues of several services, most urgent first. Its overseer registry checks attestations with expiry dates on the day, records each overseer's authority over named controls, and enforces independence (not the proposer, not the builder, not the first reviewer or anyone in their reporting line). Escalation chains, standing decisions and per-environment strength are built. Its V2 proof record holds the reviewer, roles, rationale, review duration, every vote, the verdict, the source and the catalog digest (`human/docs/02-spec.md`; `human/spec/CONTRACT.md`). Several parts are stand-ins for platform work that does not exist yet. The proof ledger is a stand-in, and every demo decision is stamped `rehearsal`, so "only `runtime` counts as evidence". A halt works within one service only. A registry file stands in for identity-provider roles. Tool-call approval was tested by calling the guardrail directly, not inside a model's tool loop. Nothing in the prototype records jurisdiction, legal entity, language or time zone; the catalog is one per service, there is no layer above the bundle, and labels are English with dates in UTC (`human/docs/08-discussion.md`).

One design question needs an explicit answer before the storyboard implies one. The research describes "a global baseline with local overlays", but that phrase comes from a WEF proposal for governments, not from firm disclosures. What firms actually document is tiering by materiality plus regulators accepting group frameworks. The product has already rejected overlays to keep one owning namespace per control. A consistent way forward, offered here as a recommendation, is **scope rather than overlay**. Country variation is already expressed as applicability by jurisdiction in the corpus. What is missing is binding that applicability to a decision's own country (taken from the request, as the gateway route already does) and stamping that country on the raised event, the proof record and the verdict. Scoping would let one bundle serve ten countries, let verdicts and Compliance be split by country, and let a German case be routed to reviewers with German attestations. It would add no second layer of controls. A related gap remains: a single decision often needs to satisfy GDPR Art. 22, AI Act Art. 14 and a national rule at once, but a raised event names exactly one control and a control names exactly one `corpus_ref`. That needs either a control that cites several sources with the strictest settings, or several events per decision.

## Conclusion

The multinational problem is less "which law applies to this system" than "which country does this decision belong to". The product answers the first question well, through per-system applicability across 38 jurisdictions. It does not yet answer the second, because no event, verdict or reviewer carries a country. The fix is a scope on the decision rather than a second layer of policy, and the gateway already holds the attribute at run time. That makes country-scoped oversight a short step from the current design, not a new product.

The research also changes what "human oversight" should mean on stage. The law has converged on a reviewer who can change the outcome. Agent practice is moving that reviewer up a level, to approving policies, plans and tiers of authority. Regulators are starting to ask for proof that the reviewers are paying attention: override rates, response times, verified training. The most distinctive thing the keynote can show is not a person pressing Approve, which 93% of people do almost automatically. It is the system measuring its own overseers and handing a named owner the decision when that oversight weakens. The akka/human prototype can show that today, labelled honestly. It is the strongest available answer to the keynote's own closing test.
